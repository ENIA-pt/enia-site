Skills · literacy, measurement and certification

The country already uses AI.
Nobody has measured
whether it knows how.

Almost four in ten Portuguese people used artificial intelligence tools in the last three months. At the same time, three in four companies that have not adopted AI say the reason is a lack of knowledge. Both facts are true at once — and that contradiction is exactly what national skills policy has to resolve.

          What these numbers say together

          Individual adoption is high and uneven by age: 81.5% among students, 76.5% among 16 to 24-year-olds, 52.3% among 35 to 44-year-olds. Business adoption is low and uneven by size: 49.1% in companies with 250 or more employees, 9.4% in those with 10 to 49.

          In other words: people entering the labour market already bring the skill, but find companies that do not know how to use it. And among the companies left outside, 74.4% cite a lack of knowledge and 57.3% a lack of clarity about the legal consequences. This is not a training problem — it is a problem of organisational literacy and legal certainty at the same time.

          02 · The obligation almost nobody knows about

          Article 4:
          AI literacy is
          already enforceable.

          Since 2 February 2025, the AI Act has required providers and deployers of AI systems to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among their staff and anyone operating those systems on their behalf.

          It is not a recommendation, it has no future deadline, and it does not depend on the system’s risk tier. It is the most cross-cutting obligation in the whole regulation — and the least complied with in Portugal, because almost nobody knows it exists.

          There is no official European AI literacy certificate. There is an obligation of result and the need to demonstrate it. That is the gap the AI Literacy Passport fills: a national, verifiable standard an organisation can present as evidence of diligence.

          04 · The skills strategy

          Three levels,
          three audiences.

          Finland trained 1% of its population through Elements of AI and turned that into national positioning. Portugal has a better starting point on adoption — and no equivalent instrument.

          Level 1

          AI Literacy Passport

          Individual certification, free and at mass scale, aligned with the Article 4 duty. Short, in European Portuguese, using examples from the national context — and issuing a record the person presents and the organisation files as evidence of diligence.

          For the whole working population · goal: mass adoption, public recognition thereafter

          Level 2

          Algorithmic governance for decision-makers

          Training for managers, technical staff and public decision-makers on what the AI Act requires, how a system is classified, how an inventory is built and what effective human oversight actually means. In partnership with universities and business associations.

          For middle and senior management · private sector and public administration

          Level 3

          Technical assessment of AI systems

          Specialist competence to audit, test and validate systems. This is the country’s most critical gap: without national assessors, Portugal imports the certification of its own AI — and pays import prices for it.

          For engineering, audit and regulators · a precondition for a credible enforcement unit

          Monitoring closes the loop: the AI CENSUS measures, the Passport trains, the Radar tracks the effect. Without measurement, a skills plan is a list of intentions with a budget.
